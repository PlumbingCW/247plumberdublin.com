Using an Infrared Camera to Detect Leaks in Dublin, CA

An infrared camera, also called a thermal imaging camera, shows differences in surface temperature as colors on a screen. Plumbers use it to find hidden leaks because water changes the temperature of the material around it. For a Dublin, CA homeowner with a stain, a warm floor or a mystery water bill, it can shorten the search.

It is not a magic tool, and it does not see through walls. This guide explains what it shows, where it works well and where it fails.

How thermal imaging finds leaks

Every surface gives off infrared energy based on its temperature. A thermal camera converts that energy to a picture. A hot water line leaking under a floor warms the surface above it, and a cold water leak or wet insulation cools a patch of wall or ceiling by evaporation.

The camera shows the pattern, a warm trail or a cool patch, and a plumber follows it. That narrows a search from a whole room to a few feet.

What thermal imaging can and cannot do

Know the limits before you rely on it.

Pairing it with other tools

A thermal camera gives a lead. A moisture meter confirms whether a material is wet, because a stain can be old and dry. Acoustic listening equipment picks up the sound of pressurized water escaping, and pressure tests show which section of pipe is losing pressure.

Used together, these methods let us open a small hole instead of a large one. Our leak detection page describes the sequence, and a hidden leak under concrete is covered on slab leak repair.

When to use thermal imaging

These are the situations where it earns its keep.

Warm spots on the floor

A hot line leaking under a slab or subfloor.

Ceiling stains

Tracing moisture back toward the source.

Wet insulation

Hidden behind drywall.

Radiant heat lines

Leaks show as cool or warm trails.

What to do after a leak is located

Once the leak is found, shut off the water to that branch if you can and take photos of the area for your records. If a wall or ceiling is wet, drying it quickly matters, because damp material can grow mold if it stays wet. Ask the plumber how long to let the area dry before it is closed up.

If the leak is on a supply line, the repair is usually a short section. If it is inside concrete or behind finished tile, the plumber explains the options and the work that comes with each, and you decide.

Reading a thermal image

A thermal image shows surface temperature as a color map. A cool patch on a wall can mean evaporating moisture, and a warm streak on a floor can mean a hot water line or heating tubing. The skill is in what is read from the image. A cool patch near a window may be a draft, and a warm streak near an appliance may be its normal heat.

A plumber confirms a thermal finding with a second method. A moisture meter checks whether the material is actually wet, and a pressure test tells whether the system is losing water. Treat a thermal image as a clue that points to where to check, not as proof by itself.

Conditions that help or hurt a scan

A scan works best when there is a temperature difference between the wet area and the dry area around it. A hot water leak is easier to see than a cold one in a home with a stable indoor temperature. Direct sun on a wall, a recently run shower and a heating cycle can all create temperature patterns that look like leaks but are not.

Because of that, the plumber may ask you not to run water or adjust the thermostat before the visit. Tell dispatch when you call about any wet spots, stains or sounds so the plumber knows where to start.

Do-it-yourself infrared options

Phone-attached thermal cameras are inexpensive and can show a hot or cold patch. They are useful for a first look. Their resolution is low, and reading the image takes practice.

A cheap camera may flag a cold spot that is just an uninsulated stud. Treat it as a clue, then confirm with a moisture reading or a meter test. If the meter runs with everything off, you have a leak, and a plumber with proper tools finds it faster.

Dublin, CA: reading your meter

In Dublin the Dublin San Ramon Services District provides water and owns the meter, so the meter test is the same for every home: turn everything off, read the dial and the leak indicator, wait two hours and read again. A change means water is escaping on your side. The district can also tell you how your usage compares over time.

Questions about this topic

Can an infrared camera see through walls?

No. It shows surface temperature. A leak behind a wall shows only if it changes the surface temperature.

Is thermal imaging accurate?

It is a useful clue, not proof. We confirm with moisture readings and listening equipment.

What does it cost to have a leak located?

It depends on the method and access. We give a clear price before work starts.

How do I check if the meter in Dublin shows a leak?

Turn off all water, read the meter and its leak indicator, wait two hours and read again.
