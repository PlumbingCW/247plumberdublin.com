Whole House Water Leak Detection System: A Dublin, CA Guide

A whole-house water leak detection system watches the flow in your supply line and shuts the water off automatically when it sees a pattern that looks like a leak. For a home in Dublin, CA, it can be a useful safeguard when you travel or when a leak could hide inside a wall. It is not a replacement for a plumber, and it does not find a leak. It only tells you one exists.

This guide explains the types of systems, what they monitor, how they are installed and what to check before buying one.

How these systems work

Most whole-house systems clamp on or install inline on the main water line. They measure flow rate and pressure continuously. A running toilet, a dripping faucet and a pinhole leak look very different from normal use, and the device compares the flow it sees with learned patterns.

If the flow runs too long or too high, the device can close a motorized valve and send an alert to your phone. Some also track water use by fixture, which helps spot a slow leak.

Types of leak detection systems

They fall into three groups, and each protects against a different problem.

Type.What it does.Limits.
Flow-based smart shutoff valve on the main.Monitors the whole house and closes the main automatically.Needs power and Wi-Fi, may need a plumber to install.
Point sensors on the floor.Alert when water touches a sensor near a heater, sink or washer.Only detect leaks at the sensor location.
Ultrasonic clamp-on flow monitor.Measures flow without cutting the pipe.Provides alerts, may not include an automatic valve.

What these systems cannot do

A system tells you water is flowing when it should not be. It does not tell you where. If a house has a slow leak under a slab, a flow monitor may flag it, but a plumber still has to locate it with listening equipment, pressure tests and thermal imaging.

Our leak detection page explains how that locating works. A system also cannot stop a leak that happens on the street side of the shutoff. And a device that loses power or Wi-Fi may not close at all, so check how it behaves when disconnected.

Installation and pressure

A smart shutoff valve is installed on the main line near the house shutoff. If the line has a pressure regulator, the valve goes after it. Installation involves cutting into the pipe, soldering or fitting the valve, and wiring it to power and a network. It is a job for a plumber because a poor installation is a leak risk of its own.

High pressure is a hidden risk. A pressure gauge on a hose bib tells you what you have. Pressure above about 80 psi stresses fittings, and a failing regulator is a frequent hidden cause of pipe bursts.

Questions to ask before buying

Not every device suits every home. Ask these first.

Does it close automatically?

Some only alert, which is useless when you are away.

What happens with no power or Wi-Fi?

The failure mode matters.

Does it need a plumber?

Most do. Be wary of products that claim you can install one without cutting the pipe.

Can I override it?

You should be able to open the valve manually if the system fails.

Who benefits most

A whole-house system suits homes that sit empty for days, vacation properties, homes with finished basements or crawlspaces and homes with water heaters inside living space. It also suits owners who want an early warning on a pipe that runs through a wall.

It suits less well when the main risk is a slow outdoor irrigation leak, which is not inside the home and may not trigger a shutoff. For that, a periodic meter check is the better tool, and a plumber can show you how to do it.

Alternatives and complements

If a smart valve is more than you want, simple steps help. A water sensor under the water heater, the washer and the kitchen sink costs little and alerts you to a leak at the source. Replacing old rubber washing machine hoses with braided ones reduces risk. Knowing where your main shutoff is, and testing it yearly, is a free safeguard.

A meter check also helps. Many meters have a leak indicator. If it spins with everything off, you have a leak. See our water service line repair page if the leak is outside.

Dublin, CA: your meter and the district

Drinking water in Dublin is provided by the Dublin San Ramon Services District, which buys wholesale water from Zone 7 Water Agency. The district owns the meter, and the pipe from the meter to the house is generally the owner's side, so a shutoff valve you install goes on your side of the meter. Ask the district where its responsibility ends and whether it can share your usage history, which helps you spot when a leak started.

Source type for this note: the Dublin San Ramon Services District website describing its water supply from Zone 7.

Questions about this topic

Does a leak detection system find the leak?

No. It detects abnormal flow and can shut the water off. A plumber still has to locate the leak.

Where does the valve go?

On the main water line near the house shutoff, usually after the pressure regulator if there is one.

Who owns the meter in Dublin, CA?

The Dublin San Ramon Services District. Confirm with the district where its responsibility ends for your address.

Can I install one myself?

It involves cutting into the main line, so it is best done by a plumber.
